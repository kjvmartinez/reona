Floating Small Modular Reactors for the Philippines:
Assessing Feasibility, Suitability, and Urgency in an Archipelagic Context

Abstract
The Philippines faces a converging energy crisis: rising demand, heavy dependence on imported coal, frequent grid alerts, and limited land for large-scale renewable expansion. This paper examines whether floating small modular reactors (SMRs) offer a feasible, suitable, and urgent solution. Drawing on policy documents, grid data, and international case studies, the analysis finds that while floating SMR technology is not yet commercially mature, its unique characteristics—high capacity factor, minimal land use, offshore siting, and modular scalability—align well with the country’s archipelagic geography and baseload needs. Renewables alone cannot match nuclear’s reliability or density, and geothermal and hydro are approaching saturation. Recent policy advances, private-sector investments, and international partnerships suggest a realistic pathway, provided that regulatory safeguards, workforce development, and public engagement proceed in parallel. The paper concludes with a phased roadmap for responsible deployment.
Keywords: floating SMR, nuclear energy, Philippines, energy security, baseload power, archipelagic grid

1. Introduction

The Philippines is at an energy crossroads. Economic growth and population expansion are driving electricity demand upward, yet the national grid remains heavily reliant on imported coal—a dependency that exposes households and industries to volatile global prices and supply disruptions. In 2025, the Visayas and Mindanao grids experienced repeated “yellow alerts,” indicating dangerously thin operating reserves. At the same time, a government moratorium on new coal plants (in place since 2020) has narrowed the options for new baseload capacity.

Against this backdrop, floating small modular reactors (SMRs) have entered policy discussions. Unlike conventional land-based nuclear plants, which require large, seismically stable sites and decade-long construction timelines, floating SMRs are factory-built, towable units of up to 300 MW each, designed to operate offshore. Russia’s Akademik Lomonosov has demonstrated the concept’s technical viability in the Arctic since 2020, and several other nations are developing commercial variants.

This paper asks three questions. First, how urgent is the need for new baseload capacity in the Philippines? Second, are floating SMRs suitable for an archipelago with high seismic activity, limited flat land, and diverse island grids? Third, given current policy and technological maturity, is deployment feasible in the near to medium term? The analysis focuses on the period 2026–2040, aligning with the Philippine Energy Plan’s targets.

2. Urgency: Why the Status Quo Is Unsustainable

The case for urgency rests on four observable trends. First, peak demand is projected to more than double from approximately 20,669 MW in 2025 to 53,679 MW by 2040 (Department of Energy [DOE], 2025). Second, coal still accounts for 56.6% of generation, and the Philippines is the most coal-dependent grid in Southeast Asia. Third, the country imports virtually all of its coal, making electricity tariffs highly sensitive to global price shocks; Philippine industrial rates are already the second highest in the region. Fourth, forced outages of coal and geothermal plants have become routine. In November 2025 alone, over 725 MW of capacity in the Visayas was unavailable, triggering yet another yellow alert (National Grid Corporation of the Philippines [NGCP], 2025).

The coal moratorium, while environmentally justified, has created a supply gap. Renewables are being added, but solar and wind are intermittent; battery storage at the scale needed for multi-day lulls remains prohibitively expensive. Geothermal production is nearing its practical limit, and large hydropower depends on seasonal rainfall patterns that are becoming less predictable. Without a new source of reliable baseload power, the Philippines faces either rolling blackouts or a backslide on coal restrictions.

Floating SMRs offer a potential timeline advantage. Deployment can be completed in four to six years from final investment decision, compared to a decade or more for conventional nuclear plants. The government’s stated goal of bringing the first 1,200 MW of nuclear capacity online by 2032 (DOE, 2025) is ambitious but not impossible if preparatory work accelerates in 2026–2027.

3. Suitability: Comparing Nuclear with Renewables and Land-Based Options

Suitability involves matching technology characteristics to the Philippines’ specific constraints: archipelagic geography, high seismic risk, limited flat land, and a grid that needs stable baseload power.

3.1 The Intermittency Problem

Solar photovoltaic systems in the Philippines achieve capacity factors of 15–25%, and wind farms 25–40%, depending on location. Floating SMRs, by contrast, achieve capacity factors above 90%—they run continuously regardless of weather or time of day. For industrial zones, data centers, and metropolitan areas, this reliability is not optional. Battery storage can smooth fluctuations over hours but cannot economically cover multi-day periods of low sun and wind, such as those brought by typhoons. Geothermal (typical capacity factor 70–85%) is more reliable but geographically constrained; most accessible sites are already developed or face opposition from indigenous communities.

3.2 Land Use and Environmental Footprint

A 300 MW floating SMR generating approximately 2,400 GWh annually would require roughly 1,200–1,500 hectares of land if replaced by solar photovoltaics, assuming 1,600 kWh per kWp-year and a 20% capacity factor. That area is equivalent to about 1,600 football fields—land that in the Philippines often means converting agricultural or forested areas. Wind farms require even more dispersed land, often on mountain ridges or offshore zones that conflict with fishing and shipping. The floating SMR itself occupies no land; its onshore footprint (switching station, administrative building) is typically less than five hectares.

3.3 Scalability and Grid Stability

SMRs are modular: a single 300 MW unit can be deployed, and additional units added as demand grows. This incremental approach avoids the financial and technical risk of overbuilding. Moreover, SMRs provide grid inertia through spinning turbines, contributing to frequency regulation without additional investment. High penetrations of inverter-based renewables (solar, wind) require synchronous condensers or battery-based synthetic inertia, adding system costs.

3.4 Seismic and Tsunami Resilience

A common concern is whether a floating reactor can survive Philippine earthquakes and tsunamis. Floating platforms are not rigidly anchored to the seabed; they can be designed with mooring systems that absorb seismic shock. In the event of a tsunami, the platform rises with the water rather than being overtopped. While no floating SMR has yet been tested in a major subduction zone earthquake, engineering analyses suggest that offshore floating structures can be more resilient than land-based plants in such settings (IAEA, 2021). This remains an area requiring site-specific validation.

4. Feasibility: Policy, Investment, and Remaining Gaps

4.1 Policy and Regulatory Progress

Several milestones have been achieved since 2020. The Philippine National Nuclear Energy Safety Act was ratified, creating the Philippine Atomic Energy Regulatory Authority (PhilATOM) as an independent regulator. The DOE released a draft circular integrating nuclear energy—including SMRs, micro-reactors, and floating plants—into the national generation mix. PhilATOM has begun developing licensing procedures aligned with International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) standards.

4.2 Private Sector and International Engagement

Manila Electric Company (Meralco), the country’s largest distribution utility, launched its Nuclear Energy Strategic Transition (NEST) program. Through the U.S. Trade and Development Agency (USTDA), Meralco received a $2.7 million grant in 2025 and a subsequent $2.8 million grant in 2026 for SMR feasibility studies. Meralco has also signed cooperation agreements with Russian (Rosatom) and South Korean (KHNP) nuclear entities. These are not token gestures; they involve site assessments, technology scanning, and workforce training components.

The DOE has identified potential nuclear facility sites in Bulacan, Batangas, Masbate, Palawan, and Bataan. The NGCP has confirmed available transmission capacity of 10,260 MW, sufficient to accommodate initial SMR deployments (NGCP, 2025).

4.3 Remaining Gaps and Risks

No commercial floating SMR is yet operating outside of Russia’s Akademik Lomonosov (which is often classified as a small floating nuclear power plant rather than an SMR in the modular sense). Philippine law requires the country of origin to demonstrate at least two years of safe, incident-free operation before the technology can be imported. This creates a waiting period. Additionally, the Philippines lacks a domestic nuclear workforce. Meralco’s FISSION program, which sends Filipino engineers abroad for training, is a start, but building a full regulatory and operational workforce will take five to ten years.

Public acceptance is another major variable. The legacy of the Bataan Nuclear Power Plant, built but never operated, combined with memories of Fukushima, fuels skepticism. Any deployment plan must include sustained, transparent community engagement and independent safety oversight.

5. Discussion: A Phased Roadmap

Given the above, a prudent approach is neither to rush nor to wait indefinitely. The following phased roadmap is proposed:

  • 2026–2027: Complete feasibility studies and technology scanning. No binding commitments. Continue workforce training and regulatory capacity-building.
  • 2028–2029: Select a technology partner based on real-world operating data (by then, several SMR designs may have achieved commercial operation in Canada, the US, or the UK). Begin pre-licensing and site-specific environmental impact assessments.
  • 2030–2032: Deploy a pilot floating SMR of 100–200 MW at a carefully selected site (e.g., Batangas or Palawan). Establish independent monitoring and public reporting.
  • 2033–2035: Evaluate pilot performance. If safety and reliability targets are met, proceed with scaled deployment (additional units, potentially other island grids).

6. Conclusion

The Philippines cannot afford to ignore floating SMRs. The urgency is real: a coal-dependent grid under growing strain, limited land for renewables, and the approaching limits of geothermal and hydro. Floating SMRs offer a unique combination of high reliability, minimal land use, offshore siting, and modular scalability that aligns with the country’s archipelagic reality. Renewables alone cannot match this potential, not because renewables are inferior, but because their intermittency and land footprint make them unsuitable as the sole baseload source.

Feasibility is improving. Policy frameworks are being built, private investment is flowing, and international partnerships are active. The remaining gaps—technology maturity, workforce, and public acceptance—are real but manageable through a phased, transparent approach. The choice is not between nuclear and renewables. The choice is between a diversified, resilient grid and continued dependence on imported coal with all its economic and environmental costs.

The time for academic debate is not over, but it must now inform concrete action. Pilot projects, regulatory sandboxes, and targeted workforce development should begin immediately, even as final technology decisions await more operational data. Energy security is too important to postpone.
