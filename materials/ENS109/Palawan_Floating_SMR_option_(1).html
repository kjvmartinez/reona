Position Paper on the Suitability and Feasibility
of a Floating Small Modular Reactor (SMR)
in Palawan, Philippines

A Strategic Energy Solution for Energy Security, Resilience, and Sustainable Development
To: Office of the Governor, Province of Palawan; Department of Energy (DOE); Philippine Nuclear Research Institute (PNRI); Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC); Palawan Electric Cooperative (PALECO); and other relevant government agencies and stakeholders
Subject: Position Paper on the Suitability and Feasibility of a Floating Small Modular Reactor (SMR) in Palawan, Philippines
Date: April 3, 2026

I. Executive Summary

This position paper presents a compelling case for the deployment of a Floating Small Modular Reactor (SMR) in the province of Palawan, Philippines. Palawan faces persistent and acute challenges in achieving a stable, affordable, and sustainable electricity supply—obstacles that directly impede its economic development and the well-being of its residents. Given the province’s unique geography as an archipelago, its environmental sensitivity as a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, and its susceptibility to natural hazards, a floating SMR emerges not only as a technically sound option but as a strategically superior alternative to traditional land-based power plants. It offers a unique combination of low-carbon baseload power, enhanced safety, minimal on-land environmental footprint, and energy independence.

II. The Imperative for Action: Palawan’s Energy Crisis

The electricity situation in Palawan remains precarious and economically burdensome. Despite being a major tourism hub and an ecologically critical region, the province suffers from chronic supply shortages, high electricity costs, and an over-reliance on expensive and polluting fossil fuels.

A. High and Volatile Electricity Costs

Residential electricity rates in Palawan have been recorded at approximately P13.93 per kilowatt-hour (kWh), significantly higher than spot market prices on the main Luzon grid. This cost burden is directly linked to the reliance on diesel and bunker fuel, the prices of which are passed directly to consumers. Such high energy costs undermine the competitiveness of local businesses and place an undue financial strain on households.

B. Supply Insecurity and Aging Infrastructure

The province’s power supply is a patchwork of diesel generators, coal-biomass plants, and small-scale renewable energy projects that have struggled to keep pace with rising demand. A 2021 editorial review of the Palawan Island Energy Development Plan (PIEDP) starkly concluded that most of its 2014–2035 targets for stable supply and grid expansion had not been realized, leaving the region trapped in recurring cycles of shortages and pricing shocks. In 2025, a 40-MW Power Supply Agreement (PSA) was activated to help stabilize the grid, but it only underscores the ongoing struggle to secure adequate capacity. Furthermore, off-grid power generator DMCI Power Corp. has proposed a P3-billion expansion of its coal-biomass plant, signaling that the current system is still insufficient to meet existing and near-future demand. This reflects a cycle of fossil fuel dependency rather than a sustainable long-term solution.

C. Unrealized Development Goals

The failure to meet the targets of the PIEDP has had tangible consequences. Key milestones, such as connecting major municipalities to the main grid and establishing submarine power cables for island interconnections, have been delayed or remain incomplete. A congressional probe was even called to investigate PALECO’s ongoing power crisis, highlighting the urgency for a transformative solution beyond incremental fixes.

D. A New Geopolitical Reality: The Vulnerability Exposed by the Iran War

The recent and sudden war in the Middle East has laid bare the extreme vulnerability of the Philippines’ energy security in the most stark and undeniable terms. The outbreak of hostilities between the US‑Israel alliance and Iran on February 28, 2026, which led to the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, has served as a brutal stress test for a nation that depends almost entirely on imported oil. The facts are sobering:

  • Overwhelming Reliance: The Philippines imports 98% of its crude oil from the Middle East, with the vast majority of those shipments passing through the Strait of Hormuz. This chokepoint is the sole maritime corridor for nearly one‑quarter of the world’s seaborne oil.
  • Economic Shock: The conflict caused global oil prices to skyrocket, with Philippine diesel and petrol prices more than doubling within weeks of the war’s onset.
  • National Emergency: Facing an imminent supply cutoff and with national fuel reserves estimated to cover less than 45 days of demand, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. declared a state of national energy emergency on March 24, 2026—the first such declaration by any nation in response to the war. The government was forced to impose a four‑day work week for civil servants and slash public ferry services to conserve rapidly dwindling fuel supplies.
  • Diplomatic Scramble: The country was forced into high‑stakes, crisis diplomacy, seeking safe passage for its fuel ships directly from Iran. It was also compelled to make an “extreme necessity” purchase of 2.5 million barrels of Russian crude oil, a transaction that would have been unthinkable before the war and which required navigating US sanctions.

This sudden geopolitical crisis has delivered a clear and urgent lesson: the Philippines cannot outsource the foundation of its economic and social stability to a single, volatile, and far‑away chokepoint. Palawan, as an island province acutely sensitive to fuel price spikes and supply disruptions, stands to be one of the greatest beneficiaries of a more resilient, domestically-sourced energy system. A floating SMR offers exactly that—a path to genuine energy independence, insulating the province and the nation from the catastrophic economic fallout of future geopolitical convulsions in the Middle East.

III. A Superior Alternative: The Case for a Floating Small Modular Reactor (SMR)

Given Palawan’s unique constraints, a floating SMR presents a paradigm-shifting solution. Unlike conventional nuclear plants, a floating SMR is factory-built, transported by barge, and moored offshore—directly addressing Palawan’s land scarcity and environmental concerns.

A. Proven and Maturing Global Technology

The concept is not theoretical. Russia’s Akademik Lomonosov, the world’s first floating nuclear power plant (FNPP), began commercial operation in 2020, reliably supplying power to the remote Arctic port of Pevek. China is actively developing similar ACPR50S reactors for island power generation. More recently, in 2025, the American Bureau of Shipping (ABS) issued an Approval in Principle (AIP) to HD Hyundai Heavy Industries for a floating SMR power module designed to support onshore communities, confirming the commercial and technical viability of such designs. International maritime classification societies are actively developing rules for floating nuclear applications, with the first SMRs for maritime use expected as early as 2030. This growing global momentum provides a clear pathway for the Philippines to adopt a technology with multiple proven and certifiable designs.

B. Palawan’s Geological Suitability and Inherent Safety Advantages

The selection of an energy infrastructure site must be guided by a rigorous assessment of geological risks. In this regard, Palawan offers a strategic advantage that is unparalleled in the Philippines. The Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS) has long identified Palawan as the only region in the country that is largely earthquake-safe, as it sits on a stable continental shelf and is not bounded by any major active fault lines. While the rest of the Philippines lies within the seismically active Philippine Mobile Belt, Palawan is situated atop the Eurasian Plate, a tectonic setting that is fundamentally more stable. Scientific data further validates this exceptional stability: from 2014 to 2017, PHIVOLCS recorded no earthquake events in Palawan, and a USGS map of earthquake activity from 1990 to 2006 shows the province experienced just one shallow earthquake over a 16-year period—in stark contrast to the hundreds of tremors recorded across the rest of the nation. Geologically, Palawan is composed of thick continental rocks that extend up to 30 kilometers below the surface, providing a foundation of “relative geological stability” that is ideal for critical infrastructure. For a floating SMR, this stability is not merely a benefit but a core component of its safety case. Because the plant is floating, seismic loads from the ocean floor do not transfer to the reactor structures—earthquakes are completely eliminated as a safety concern. The platform’s ability to ride out any long-period ground motion makes Palawan’s already low seismological hazard profile an even more compelling endorsement for this technology.

C. Minimal Land-Use and Environmental Footprint

Palawan’s designation as a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve makes land-based industrial development highly contentious. A floating SMR overcomes this hurdle entirely. It requires no large-scale land acquisition, no permanent on-land exclusion zone, and no irreversible alteration of the terrestrial environment. This design sidesteps the most significant political and environmental obstacles that have long stalled other energy projects in the province.

D. The Inadequacy of Renewable Energy Alternatives

While renewable energy sources like solar, wind, and hydropower are essential components of a sustainable energy mix, relying on them as the primary solution for Palawan’s baseload power requirements is neither practical nor reliable. Solar and wind energy are inherently intermittent, dependent on weather conditions and the time of day, which creates significant challenges for grid stability and reliability. To provide a constant supply of electricity, a baseload power source—capable of operating continuously 24 hours a day, 7 days a week—is crucial for balancing the grid. Palawan has already experienced the consequences of over-reliance on a fragmented energy mix. The Palawan Island Energy Development Plan (PIEDP), a master plan crafted by the Department of Energy for 2014–2035, explicitly envisioned that by 2017, the province would achieve a stable supply through a mix of fossil fuels and renewable sources like hydropower, solar, and wind. Yet, the province has persistently failed to meet those targets, with key milestones for grid expansion and renewable integration remaining unfulfilled. This track record underscores that intermittent renewables alone cannot provide the reliable, 24/7 baseload power that Palawan urgently needs. Furthermore, while geothermal energy offers a renewable baseload alternative, Palawan has no proven high-temperature geothermal resource suitable for large-scale electricity generation. The province lacks the volcanic activity that typically drives geothermal fields, making geothermal an impractical solution. Wind resources in the region are less consistent than solar, and hydropower is constrained by seasonal variations and environmental concerns over river systems. A floating SMR, by contrast, provides a reliable, low-carbon baseload power source that is immune to weather variability, making it the ideal complement to a diversified renewable energy portfolio rather than a competitor to it.

E. Alignment with National Policy

This proposal is fully aligned with the Philippines’ national energy strategy. The Department of Energy (DOE) has set a target of adding 1,200 MW of nuclear power by 2032 and is preparing to accept applications for nuclear projects. Leading energy player Meralco has launched a US-backed SMR Adoption Study, actively evaluating potential sites. The DOE has also specifically identified Palawan as a candidate site for future nuclear facilities, recognizing its geographic isolation and low population density as strategic advantages. A floating SMR represents the most direct and practical path for Palawan to contribute to this national goal.

IV. Addressing Key Concerns and Risks

We acknowledge the legitimate concerns surrounding nuclear energy and have addressed them as follows:

ConcernMitigation and Response
Environmental ImpactThe reactor is a zero-carbon energy source. Its offshore location and robust containment design virtually eliminate the risk of contamination to land-based or marine ecosystems under normal or accident conditions.
Safety and SecurityAs detailed in Section III.B, floating SMRs are designed with multiple layers of passive safety systems and natural security buffers, offering a safety profile superior to many existing land-based power plants.
Regulatory FrameworkThe Philippines is actively developing its nuclear regulatory framework. The DOE has finalized a licensing and permitting flowchart, and the proposed Philippine Atomic Energy Regulatory Authority (PhilATOM) will ensure all projects meet the highest IAEA standards.
Waste ManagementA floating SMR’s reactor can be designed to operate for extended periods (e.g., 10–25 years) without refueling. At the end of its life, the entire barge can be towed to a centralized facility for decommissioning and waste processing, preventing waste storage from becoming a local burden.

V. Strategic Recommendations

To advance this proposal, we urge the following actions:

  1. Pre-Feasibility Study: The DOE, in coordination with PALECO and international partners (e.g., IAEA, USTDA), should immediately commission a pre-feasibility study for a floating SMR in Palawan. This study should assess specific mooring locations, grid interconnection points, and economic models.
  2. Regulatory Preparation: The Philippine government must accelerate the establishment of the PhilATOM and finalize comprehensive regulations for floating nuclear plants, which fall under a distinct category from traditional land-based facilities.
  3. Public Engagement and Education: A transparent and sustained information campaign must be launched to educate Palaweños and local government officials on the advanced safety features, economic benefits, and minimal environmental risks of floating SMR technology. Stakeholder buy-in is essential.
  4. International Collaboration: The Philippines should actively engage with nations at the forefront of floating SMR development—including Russia, China, South Korea, and the United States—to leverage their technical expertise and operational experience.

VI. Conclusion

The status quo of Palawan’s energy sector is untenable. The path of expanding fossil fuel reliance is a road to continued economic leakage, environmental degradation, and climate vulnerability. The intermittent and limited nature of purely renewable solutions cannot provide the reliable baseload power that Palawan urgently requires. In contrast, the convergence of three decisive factors—Palawan’s exceptionally low seismological hazard and geological stability, the inherent safety advantages of offshore siting, and the technological maturity of floating SMR designs—makes this the optimal, most responsible, and most sustainable energy solution for the province. This is not merely an energy proposal; it is a strategic vision for the sustainable and prosperous future of the Province of Palawan and its people. We respectfully request the inclusion of this proposal in the national energy agenda and a formal evaluation of its potential for the province.
