What is Sustainable Development?

Sustainable development meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, 1987).

Three pillars:

  • Environmental protection – climate, biodiversity, water, air.
  • Social equity – health, education, inclusion, human rights.
  • Economic development – jobs, growth, innovation.
4

Link to the SDGs

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) operationalize sustainable development across 17 goals (e.g., poverty, energy, climate).

  • Goal 12 – Responsible Consumption and Production.
  • Goal 13 – Climate Action.
  • Goal 8 – Decent Work and Economic Growth.

These are closely tied to circular‑economy and inclusive‑growth strategies. [web:5][web:6]

What is Inclusive Economic Growth?

Inclusive growth raises average incomes and broadly distributes opportunities and benefits across society.

It emphasizes:

  • Quality jobs and decent work.
  • Reduced inequality (income and access).
  • Investment in human capital: education, health, digital skills.
6

Key Features of Inclusive Growth

  • Broad participation in the labor market and entrepreneurship.
  • Support for MSMEs, cooperatives, and informal‑sector workers.
  • Pro‑poor public investment in infrastructure, education, and healthcare.
  • Digital inclusion and financial‑access programs.

7

Policy Tools for Inclusion

  • Progressive taxation and social protection.
  • Labor‑market reforms (minimum wage, safety nets, skills training).
  • Targeted support for women, youth, and marginalized groups.
  • Access to finance and business development services for micro‑enterprises.

Linear vs Circular Economy

Linear economy:

“Take → Make → Dispose” model with high resource use and waste.

Circular economy:

  • Keeps products, components, and materials at their highest value for as long as possible.
  • Minimizes waste and pollution.
  • Decouples growth from resource depletion. [web:1][web:8]
9

Core Principles of Circular Economy

  • Reduce resource use and waste.
  • Reuse products and components.
  • Recycle and Recover materials.
  • Rethink business models and design.
  • Regenerate natural systems (soil, water, forests). [web:1][web:3][web:9]
10

Examples of Circular‑Economy Practices

  • Product‑as‑a‑service (leasing instead of selling).
  • Industrial symbiosis: waste from one firm becomes input for another. [web:7][web:8]
  • Design for durability, repairability, and recyclability.
  • Urban systems: waste‑to‑energy, composting, water reuse. [web:1][web:7]
11

How Circular Economy Supports Sustainable Development

  • Reduces pressure on ecosystems by lowering resource extraction and pollution. [web:6][web:8]
  • Creates green jobs in recycling, repair, and remanufacturing (SDG 8). [web:6][web:10]
  • Supports SDG 12 and SDG 13 through efficiency gains. [web:6][web:8]
12

Environmental and Economic Benefits

  • Higher resource efficiency and lower emissions per unit of output. [web:8][web:9]
  • Lower long‑term costs for raw materials and waste management.
  • Increased innovation and competitiveness for firms adopting circular models. [web:2][web:6]
13

Social and Equity Considerations

  • Circular economy can foster inclusive growth when jobs and contracts reach informal‑sector workers and marginalized groups. [web:3][web:6]
  • Risks: informal waste pickers may be excluded if formalization is not inclusive.
  • Green jobs often require new skills, so training and social policies are essential. [web:3][web:6]
14

How They Fit Together

  • Sustainable development – overarching goal.
  • Inclusive economic growth – engine that ensures broad participation.
  • Circular economy – economic model that aligns growth with environmental limits. [web:5][web:6][web:8]
15

Integrated Policy Strategies

  • National and local circular‑economy roadmaps aligned with SDG frameworks. [web:1][web:6]
  • Support SMEs in adopting circular and low‑carbon technologies.
  • Urban planning: circular cities (waste, mobility, buildings, water). [web:1][web:7]
16

Business and Community Roles

  • Firms: redesign products, adopt circular business models, measure KPIs. [web:2][web:9]
  • Communities: repair cafés, local recycling hubs, co‑owned systems.
  • Civil society: monitor equity impacts and advocate for inclusive transitions. [web:3][web:6]
17

Key Challenges

  • Path dependence on linear systems (infrastructure, subsidies, consumer habits). [web:5][web:8]
  • Trade‑offs between speed of transition, competitiveness, and social protection.
  • Risk of “green‑washing” or circularity that benefits large firms while excluding smaller actors. [web:3][web:6]
18

Discussion Prompts

  • Can economic growth be truly “inclusive” and “sustainable” under current global structures? Why or why not?
  • Who wins and who potentially loses in a circular‑economy transition?
  • How can developing countries balance growth needs with circular‑economy ambitions? [web:5][web:6]
19

Take‑Home Messages

  • Sustainable development integrates environment, society, and economy. [web:5][web:8]
  • Inclusive economic growth ensures that prosperity is widely shared. [web:5]
  • The circular economy is a practical model that can make growth more sustainable and inclusive—but only if designed with equity in mind. [web:3][web:6][web:8]
